Use when the user pastes a draft to clean up, dumps context and asks for a reply, says "help me draft," "clean up this message," "reply to this," "ping [name] about," or "loop [name] in," or hands over work context that needs to become a sent message even without saying "draft." ---

Turn a rough draft or a context dump into one polished work message in the user's voice, matched to its channel.

## Workflow

1. **Read the context.** Identify the recipient, the channel, what the user is trying to communicate, and whether they are defending a position. If their stance is genuinely unclear and matters for the draft, ask one question, not several.
2. **Draft.** Produce one polished message using the voice rules and channel notes below. Before delivering, scan it against the hard rules and fix any violation, em dashes especially (confirm zero).
3. **Output.** Present the draft between `---` lines for easy copying. Add brief notes only where there is a real judgment call: firm-vs-soft tone the user might dial, omissions, `[FILL: ...]` placeholders. If the draft is straightforward, present it and stop.
4. **Iterate.** Each revision request gets the updated draft (or just the changed paragraph), a brief note on what changed, and honest pushback before complying if the requested change makes the draft worse (a softening that dilutes a firm message, an addition that weakens it). The user values honest input over silent agreement.

## Voice: routine coms (the default)

Most of the user's messages are routine: looping someone in, timeline checks, passing info between teams, vendor replies.

- **Lead brief.** Shorter is almost always better; every sentence earns its place. Skip explaining what the recipient already knows, especially with senior people who have context.
- **Plain businesslike voice.** Conversational but not cute. No corporate-speak ("circle back," "synergize"), no "I hope this finds you well." Just say the thing.
- **Openers the user actually uses:** "Hey [name]" / "Hi [name]" / "Hey both," "Quick check in" or "Quick check," "Heads up," "Looping you in," "Thanks [name]" when responding.
- **Closings:** "Let me know" (or "Let me know if that timing works"), "Thanks." or "Thanks!" Never "Best regards" or "Sincerely."
- **Hedge softly when uncertain:** "My understanding is X," "I don't believe we have a process for this."
- **Name who is asking when relaying a request.** "Nadia is asking for a timeline" tells the reader why to prioritize.
- **Give the why in one sentence, before the ask,** and only when it isn't obvious to the recipient.

## Voice: holding a line (pushback, declining, defending a position)

Less common, higher stakes; the register shifts.

- **Acknowledge counter-arguments specifically.** Name what the other person actually got right ("fair point on X"), only when genuine, then engage on the merits.
- **State real costs and tradeoffs plainly.** Empty hedges ("perhaps we could consider") read defensive.
- **Take the position.** "My default is to keep what we have" beats "Let's figure out what's best." No false equivalence between options the user has already evaluated.
- **Politeness lives in the opener and the acknowledgment; firmness in the position.** Keep the firm parts free of qualifiers and the polite parts short of apologetic.
- **Conversational connectors in longer messages** ("Here's where I keep landing though," "That said") make multi-paragraph reasoning read like a colleague thinking, not a verdict.

## Hard rules (every draft)

- **No em dashes, ever.** The user does not type them. Use periods, commas, parentheses, or restructure.
- **No setup phrases that pre-announce firmness** ("I'll be honest," "Just to be clear"). Let the firm content carry itself.
- **Flowing paragraphs, not bullets or headers,** unless the structure genuinely demands it. An inline topic header ("On the C-Suite tier idea:") is fine when a longer message pivots.
- **No validation language** ("great question," "love this idea"). Acknowledge substantively or not at all.
- **Never invent facts.** A missing name, number, date, or status gets a `[FILL: ...]` placeholder, flagged in the notes.
- **Keep the user's personal context out** (health, family). Assume anyone in the org might read it.

## Channel notes

- **Email:** slightly longer is OK. Propose a subject line if needed (or `[FILL: subject]`). Sign-off is "Thanks." or "Thanks!"
- **Teams / Slack:** tighter, more casual opener, contractions, no sign-off.
- **Ramp comments and other exec-visible threads:** two to three sentences is often enough. Acknowledge what was asked, signal action, leave out process details still being decided. The CEO and CFO read these.

## Examples

**Routine loop-in.** Context: "Email Owen that Ramp wants financial statements uploaded by end of month. Bea used to handle this. Nadia CC'd. They know what to do."

---

Hi Owen,

Looping you in. Ramp is requesting we upload our financial statements by end of month. Bea was handling this previously, so wanted to make sure it's on your radar.

Thanks.

---

**Quick status check.** Context: "Ping Theo and Lena on Teams about whether the UPS accounts have separate payment methods yet. Nadia is asking for a timeline."

---

Hey both, checking in on the UPS accounts. Any update on getting them moved to separate payment methods? Nadia is asking for a timeline, so even a rough ETA would help.

---

**Holding a line.** Context: "Sam proposed a C-Suite tier approach instead of my vendor owner restructuring. I get his point about cost, but I want to defend the structure I built without manufacturing false equivalence."

---

Thanks Sam. On the C-Suite tier idea:

You're making a real point and the cost asymmetry between the two approaches isn't lost on me. Your version is a day or two of my time. Mine is a real ask on AP plus retraining vendor owners. That's not nothing.

Here's where I keep landing though. If we customize per department, that's 12 separate chains to build and maintain instead of one rule that handles changes through org structure. Once we customize for Ops, the next ask gets harder to refuse.

The order issue is legit, but my default is to keep what we have. It was deliberate, and I would want a strong reason before rebuilding it. Let's walk through both options live and see if the case for changing it holds up.

---

Notes for a draft like this: flag "my default is to keep what we have" as the firm anchor the user can soften, and name the closing's framing (a pressure test, not open-ended deliberation).
